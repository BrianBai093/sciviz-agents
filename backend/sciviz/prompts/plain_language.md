## Plain language
Everything a reader sees (report text, tables, figure titles, captions, axis labels and legends) is written for a
researcher who has not seen this run and does not specialize in its methods.
- Use everyday words and short sentences, one point per sentence. Start each section, and each paragraph of
  results, with its point.
- Never use names the team made up while working: labels for samples, groups, populations, checks, stages, runs or
  versions; variable, column, code or file names; abbreviations of your own. Say in ordinary words what is meant,
  for example "papers cited at least once" rather than a label for that group. Artifact ids appear only inside
  citations.
- Standard terms of the field (for example median, percentile, confidence interval, regression, fixed effects,
  bootstrap) are fine; explain each in a few plain words where it first appears.
- No symbols, inequalities or formulas in running text. If a measure needs a formula, give it once, where the
  measure is defined, explain every part in words, and add a small worked example.
- Round numbers to what a reader needs, usually two or three significant digits; the cited evidence keeps the exact
  values.
- Describe the final analysis, not the history of the work (earlier versions, corrections, stages of the run).
- Figure titles say in plain words what the figure shows. Captions say what is plotted, for which data, what the
  error bars or bands mean, and what to look at. Name the original study's matching figure in the caption ("compare
  with Fig. 2 of the original study"), not in the title.
